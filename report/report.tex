\title{Predicting Indoor Location from Wi-Fi Signal Strength:\\
A Phase 1 Baseline on UJIIndoorLoc}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Aviral Mishra, Ved Pawar, Khuswant Rajpurohit,
Prataya Silla, Vansh Jain}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Project 9 --- Applied Machine Learning}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We estimate where a phone is --- which building, which floor, and its coordinates --- from Wi-Fi signal strengths. On UJIIndoorLoc, we build separate tree models for each target using the supplied train/validation split. Random Forest reads the building almost perfectly (99.9\%) and gives the tightest coordinates (6.86~m median); XGBoost is the better floor classifier (92.0\%). Routing each task to whichever model wins it yields a post-hoc combined baseline at 11.58~m mean / 6.86~m median Euclidean positioning error (11.95~m mean / 7.00~m median under our penalized combined metric). Wrong-floor cases have larger coordinate errors, and errors vary more than three-fold across handsets. These results set the bar for a proposed shared multitask network.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
indoor localization, Wi-Fi fingerprinting, UJIIndoorLoc, Random Forest, XGBoost, multitask learning.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Satellite positioning stops working once you walk indoors, so phones fall back
on whatever signals are around --- and Wi-Fi is everywhere. The idea behind
fingerprinting is simple: record, at many known spots, how loud each nearby
access point sounds, then match a new reading against that map. UJIIndoorLoc is
the standard benchmark for this. It covers three university buildings with
several floors each, and every scan lists the received signal strength (RSSI)
of up to 520 access points.

What makes the problem interesting is that it is really three problems stacked
together. You want the building (a 3-way choice), the floor (5 possibilities),
and a pair of coordinates in metres. These are not independent --- knowing the
building narrows the floors, knowing the floor constrains the coordinates. This
report is Phase~1: a classical machine-learning baseline that treats each of
the three as its own model and measures how far that gets us. It is meant to be
honest and reproducible rather than clever. Phase~2 will replace it with a
network that learns the three targets jointly; the point of a baseline is to
know what ``jointly'' has to improve on.

\section{Data and Exploration}
The training file holds 19{,}937 scans and the separate validation file holds
1{,}111. We use that split as given. Because the validation scans were gathered
later and on different phones, it is a genuine test of generalisation rather
than a reshuffle of the same recordings, and we never fit any transform on it.

A first cleaning pass found 637 training scans that were exact copies of another
row --- same signals, same labels --- so we dropped them and kept 19{,}300 unique
scans. The first thing exploration makes obvious is how little each scan actually
sees: the median fingerprint picks up only 17 of the 520 access points
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sparsity}), and 96.5\% of the whole signal matrix is empty.
Fifty-five access points are never heard anywhere in training, so they carry no
information and we drop their columns.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Preparing the signals}
UJIIndoorLoc marks an access point that was not heard with the value $+100$,
which is not a real signal level and would wreck any distance or scaling if
left alone. We remap it to $-105$~dBm, just below the weakest genuine reading
($-104$), so ``not heard'' becomes a consistent floor value. The observed RSSI
is then standardised using statistics from the training data only, and the same
transform is applied unchanged to the validation set --- so the validation data
is free to drift rather than being forced to look normalised.

\subsection{Features}
On top of the 465 surviving RSSI columns we add 465 binary absence flags, one
per access point. Standard scaling is invertible: weak-but-heard and absent RSSI
remain distinct. Trees use axis-aligned splits; binary flags explicitly expose
AP visibility through a boolean threshold. They are a representation choice, not
proof of saved tree depth or improved accuracy. We also compute per-scan
summaries: how many access points were heard, the strongest reading and its AP,
and the mean, spread and range of heard signals. The design matrix has 936
columns. Everything here is cheap to compute and easy to explain.

\subsection{Models}
For each of the three targets we train both a Random Forest and an XGBoost
model: classifiers for building and floor, regressors for the two coordinates
(predicted together). That is six estimators in total, each ignorant of the
others --- which is exactly the gap Phase~2 is meant to close. Hyperparameters
are left at sensible fixed values; we are not tuning a competition entry, we are
drawing a baseline.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Classification and position}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the full picture. Random Forest misses 1 building label
in 1{,}111 scans. XGBoost reaches 91.99\% floor accuracy against RF's 90.73\%,
while RF gives tighter coordinates (6.86~m median versus 10.06~m). Routing
building and coordinates from RF and floor from XGBoost gives 11.58~m mean /
6.86~m median Euclidean error, with P90 24.99~m. The penalized score is 11.95~m
mean / 7.00~m median. It adds 4~m for a wrong floor and 50~m for a wrong
building, once each. This combination is selected post-hoc on the same validation
set, not a fresh test.

In addition to 2D Euclidean distance, we explicitly compute coordinate MAE. RF
achieves 8.19~m on Longitude and 6.80~m on Latitude (7.49~m average MAE),
substantially outperforming XGBoost's 9.82~m and 8.54~m (9.18~m average MAE).

The floor confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows that most errors are
off-by-one, on the floor directly above or below the true floor. This is
consistent with signal propagation between adjacent floors, but does not
establish its cause. The rare Floor~4 is recalled 79.5\% of the time by XGBoost
and 71.8\% by RF. No verified class-reweighting result is available for this
baseline.

\subsection{Where the error lives}
Plotting the coordinate error at each true location (Fig.~\ref{fig:errmap})
shows it is not spread evenly. Corridors with dense fingerprint coverage are
accurate; the large errors sit at building edges and corners where fewer access
points reach. The error CDF (Fig.~\ref{fig:cdf}) makes the model comparison
clean --- Random Forest's curve sits to the left of XGBoost's at every
percentile, so it is not just better on average but better across the whole
distribution of coordinate errors.

One number ties the tasks together. With RF floor predictions, mean RF coordinate
error is 10.19~m when the floor is correct and 25.21~m when it is wrong. This is
an association, not evidence that a classification error causes regression error:
the separate estimators do not pass predictions to each other. Shared learning
is a Phase~2 hypothesis to test.

\subsection{Robustness across devices}
Averages hide a lot. The validation set uses eleven phone models, of which two
(Phones 13 and 14, comprising 397 scans) were seen in training, while nine
(714 scans) are entirely unseen. Fig.~\ref{fig:robust} shows RF mean errors from
under 8~m to about 31~m, roughly four to one. One phone is poor for both models.
XGBoost, while less accurate on average, is a little more even across devices.
Device sensitivity matters for deployment and is not revealed by one mean error.

\section{Discussion and Next Steps}
The baseline does its job: building is essentially solved, floors land in the
low nineties, and median positioning sits around 7~m, from models that are easy
to reason about and quick to retrain. We report on one supplied split, not
repeated folds, so the figures are point estimates, not confidence intervals.
We did no hyperparameter search. Saved USERID-grouped folds were created but
are not consumed by the current training script. The three target models never
talk to each other.

That last point is the hand-off to Phase~2. The association between floor and
coordinate errors, and uneven behaviour across devices, motivate testing a
shared representation: one trunk feeding building, floor and coordinate heads.
Whether it helps remains to be measured. We will keep the same preprocessing,
features, split and metrics for a direct comparison.
